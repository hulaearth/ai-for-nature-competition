Modeling tables: learn from known examples

Think of these files as connected spreadsheets. They bring together the locations, numbers describing their environment, and records of the birds observed there. A model is a program that learns patterns from those examples so it can make predictions for other places.

Each location has 289 environmental numbers, often called features. They describe the place itself and its surroundings using four sources:

  • AlphaEarth: a description learned from Earth-observation data. It represents a landscape as a list of 64 numbers, called an embedding. The numbers work together; an individual number is not a measurement such as “percentage of forest.”
  • WorldCover: information about land cover, such as the kinds of surfaces found in the landscape.
  • Terrain: information derived from elevation data, describing the height and shape of the surface.
  • OpenStreetMap: information derived from historical maps of the surroundings.

The AlphaEarth inputs describe the local 10 m cell and summarize the area within 100 m and 1,000 m of it. This lets a model consider both the immediate habitat and the wider landscape. Use the prepared challenge files; the source links above explain the data and are not a request to collect extra inputs.

The default starter uses only the 64 AlphaEarth values summarized over a 1 km radius. The other feature groups stay available for explicit comparisons; you do not need all 289 to start.

Learn on one set, check on another

The package separates training examples, used to teach the model, from development examples, used to check how well it works on examples it did not learn from. Every team uses the same development set, making results comparable. The starter code helps connect the files and run a first model.

A bird record means that someone reported that species. The comparison data includes other recording opportunities, called backgrounds. These are not confirmed absences: a missing record does not prove that no bird was there. Where people look and how easily they detect birds also affect the data.

Final prediction tables: apply what your model learned

These files describe 15,915 anonymous locations using the same types of environmental numbers. Their bird observations are kept hidden so the organizers can evaluate your predictions. Your model produces a score for each of the ten target species at each location: 159,150 predictions in total.

Save the supplied identifiers and your scores in a CSV, a plain-text table with two columns: row_id,score. Include exactly the IDs from the decrypted sample submission, in any order. Scores must be finite numbers between 0 and 1, and the CSV must be at most 24 MB. Higher scores rank locations as more suitable relative to other locations; a score of 0.73 does not mean a 73% chance that the bird is present. Follow the starter instructions and submit your finished CSV from Submit.

Bird exploration dataset: investigate a wider question

This optional collection contains 2,490,462 German bird observations across all available months. Use it to explore questions such as how reports vary by season or where observations are concentrated. A concentration of reports can also reflect where people spend time observing birds.

The main modeling package already contains the labels needed for the core challenge. Save additional exploration for when your main submission and presentation are ready.

German spatial patches: keep the landscape's layout

A patch is a small grid of environmental data cut from a larger map. The modeling tables summarize an area; patches keep the position of each cell. They let a model use the arrangement of the surrounding landscape as well as its overall characteristics.

Each location has two views: a detailed view covering 1.28 × 1.28 km, and a wider view covering 2.56 × 2.56 km. Choose these optional files if your approach needs that spatial arrangement. You can complete the challenge using the prepared tables alone.

Final spatial patches: the matching inputs for prediction

If you train a model with German patches, it needs equivalent inputs for the final locations. This encrypted download supplies the same two patch sizes for every final location. Download it with the final prediction tables before the 13:30 key release.

Final locations use anonymous identifiers. You receive the supplied windows around them, not a continuous map of the test region or absolute coordinates. Check that your model can make its final predictions from these windows.

German environmental rasters: work with larger maps

A raster is a map made of a grid of cells, each storing data. These 87 files contain AlphaEarth values across Germany and a small surrounding buffer. They use GeoTIFF, a file format that keeps each grid connected to its position on a map.

At 148.57 GB, this is the largest optional download. Choose it only if your approach needs to prepare inputs from large maps; the tables and patches are already prepared for you. Final prediction still has to work with the supplied final tables or patches.

The same raster files are also available from the public Hugging Face AlphaEarth mirror if Drive has download problems. Download only the tiles you need and check them against its SHA256SUMS file. Tables, patches and encrypted final inputs are in the challenge Drive folder.

Feature counts, labels and validation

Environmental inputs

The compact input has 289 predictors: AlphaEarth contributes 195, WorldCover 39, terrain 19 and historical OpenStreetMap 36. The starter joins the companion tables. AlphaEarth's 195 values comprise three 64-number vectors (the local 10 m cell and circular summaries within 100 m and 1,000 m), plus three coverage fractions describing available data. Circular summaries combine an area; they are not single pixels at coarser resolutions.

AlphaEarth uses the 2024 representation, WorldCover is from 2021, and OpenStreetMap is from January 2024. Individual embedding coordinates do not have standalone ecological meanings. Keep the separate source notices and follow the packaged schema for exact field and coverage definitions.

Bird labels and evaluation splits

The target tables contain 83,172 training labels and 19,770 German development labels. Another 74,142 training labels cover eleven optional helper species. Five source/block/season-matched recording-opportunity backgrounds accompany each selected positive. Missing labels remain unknown, not negative labels. The exploration archive covers 492 species identifiers and 15 datasets.

Report the shared German development comparison. Five spatial folds are optional checks of how results vary across areas. Respect the supplied train, validation and purged roles across target and helper models and any preprocessing learned from data.

Spatial files, decoding and storage

German patches cover 38,374 locations in 210 compressed NPZ shards, with masks and an index. The local patch has 64 channels × 128 × 128 cells at 10 m; the wider patch has 64 channels × 64 × 64 cells at 40 m, using Google's normalized overview. Both scales are supplied for every final location with matching channels, masks and focal offsets.

Decode valid int8 values using sign(q) × (q / 127.5)²; −128 means NoData. Never average the encoded int8 values directly. Follow the packaged spatial schema for normalization, masks, axes, focal cells and offsets. National GeoTIFFs retain their source coordinate reference system and south-up transforms; patches are north-up. The 87 German rasters use a Germany-plus-2 km buffer.

The German tables, patches and exploration mirror totals 34.30 GB. The full optional collection is about 196.90 GB. A table-only final model needs the 28.2 MB encrypted table archive; optional patches bring the core test component to 13.84 GB. Models trained from whole German rasters must compute final features from the supplied windows; continuous georeferenced test-region rasters are not supplied. Keep a tested table baseline as a fallback.

Download the encrypted files onto the actual prediction runtime before 13:30. The unlock key is an age identity file. You can check the age tool with unrelated synthetic files before kickoff; check the supplied starter helper once it opens.
