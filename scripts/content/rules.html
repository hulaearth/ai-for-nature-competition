What does the prediction score measure?

Average precision (AP) measures how well your model ranks recorded bird observations above the supplied background examples. Higher is better. We calculate a score for each of the ten species, then average them equally. This average is called macro AP. Background examples are a comparison group, not proof that a bird was absent; unknown labels are excluded.

The public leaderboard and the final result

During the event, the leaderboard shows your chosen submission's AP on the public part of the test. This is provisional feedback. Final model points use the same chosen prediction on a geographically separate, hidden private part. Private scores and group details stay hidden until choices are locked, pending submissions are processed and organizers publish the results.

The AP leaderboard ranks prediction performance. The overall award adds the jury's points for insights, presentation and reproducibility. The jury does not select your best hidden result from earlier attempts.

How AP becomes model points

The supplied starter model is the fixed baseline for comparison. Matching its AP on the private test earns 15 out of 50 model points. Beating it by 0.15 AP earns all 50 points; smaller improvements earn proportionally fewer points. Scores below the baseline can earn 0–15 points. Another team's result does not change this scale.

Detailed rules and checklists

Use these notes when building your model, preparing the handoff or checking an edge case.

Validation, preprocessing and the final model

Report AP on the required German development split using the supplied labels and exclusions. This is the shared held-out check for comparing approaches before the final test.

  • Fit learned preprocessing only on the permitted training data in each partition. This includes scaling, filling missing values, dimensionality reduction, feature selection and supervised auxiliary stages. Do not refit preprocessing to the anonymous test batch.
  • Apply the same spatial and source exclusions across species. Additional released German labels must follow the documented season, quality, site and buffer rules.
  • Optional five-fold spatial cross-validation is useful for comparing promising models. It is not required and earns no points just for being run.
  • To claim that using other species helps, compare results with and without them using identical target labels, backgrounds, splits and feature supports.
  • After choosing your model, you may refit it on German training plus development data. Declare this and retain the earlier held-out development score.

Exact AP calculation, model-point formula and ties

Within each public or private partition, pool all scored countries for each species, calculate AP and average the ten species equally. The background ratio and eligibility rules are fixed. Unknown labels are excluded, not turned into negatives. Geographic identifiers and public/private membership are absent from test inputs. Submitted scores are ranking outputs, not calibrated probabilities that a bird occupies a location.

Let s be your chosen prediction's private AP, a = 1/6 the constant-score AP, and b the fixed baseline's AP on that same private split.

  • For s ≤ b: max(0, 15 × (s − a) / (b − a)) model points.
  • For s > b: min(50, 15 + 35 × (s − b) / 0.15) model points.

At or below constant-score AP, you earn zero model points. Points are capped at 50.

What you can conclude from the results

Bird records depend on where people look, what they detect and what they report. Coordinates can be less precise than the image pixels used by the model. A high prediction score does not prove that a bird occupies or breeds at a location.

The annual 2024 inputs support looking back at seasonal reported presence; they do not establish a forecast or a cause-and-effect relationship. AlphaEarth's pretraining already includes biodiversity supervision. Finding species in similar places alone does not prove that they interact. Explain these limitations when presenting your findings.
