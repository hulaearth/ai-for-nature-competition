THE MAIN CHALLENGE / 01

Where could birds thrive?

Birds offer clues about the health of our ecosystems. We chose this challenge to explore how AI can help us understand the habitats they depend on.

Why birds and why habitat?

Every species needs food, water, shelter and space to survive and reproduce. Some birds depend on forests, others on wetlands or open grasslands. Their different needs connect bird observations to the conditions of the surrounding landscape.

At Hula Earth, we want to turn that connection into useful insights for understanding and protecting biodiversity: where could suitable habitat exist, and what can environmental data tell us about it?

Your task: predict habitat suitability

Train a model on German bird observations and the supplied environmental features. Use what it learns to rank anonymous locations in neighboring Central Europe for ten bird species. Run inference on your own compute and submit your predictions; we evaluate them against hidden observation labels.

Observations also reflect where people look and how easily birds are detected. The benchmark ranks reported seasonal presence against matched recording opportunities. Backgrounds are not confirmed absences, and a high score does not establish occupancy or breeding.

Modeling tables: learn from known examples

Think of these files as connected spreadsheets. They bring together the locations, numbers describing their environment, and records of the birds observed there. A model is a program that learns patterns from those examples so it can make predictions for other places.

Each location has 289 environmental numbers, often called features. They describe the place itself and its surroundings using four sources:

  • AlphaEarth: a description learned from Earth-observation data. It represents a landscape as a list of 64 numbers, called an embedding. The numbers work together; an individual number is not a measurement such as “percentage of forest.”
  • WorldCover: information about land cover, such as the kinds of surfaces found in the landscape.
  • Terrain: information derived from elevation data, describing the height and shape of the surface.
  • OpenStreetMap: information derived from historical maps of the surroundings.

The AlphaEarth inputs describe the local 10 m cell and summarize the area within 100 m and 1,000 m of it. This lets a model consider both the immediate habitat and the wider landscape. Use the prepared challenge files; the source links above explain the data and are not a request to collect extra inputs.

The default starter uses only the 64 AlphaEarth values summarized over a 1 km radius. The other feature groups stay available for explicit comparisons; you do not need all 289 to start.

Learn on one set, check on another

The package separates training examples, used to teach the model, from development examples, used to check how well it works on examples it did not learn from. Every team uses the same development set, making results comparable. The starter code helps connect the files and run a first model.

A bird record means that someone reported that species. The comparison data includes other recording opportunities, called backgrounds. These are not confirmed absences: a missing record does not prove that no bird was there. Where people look and how easily they detect birds also affect the data.

Final prediction tables: apply what your model learned

These files describe 15,915 anonymous locations using the same types of environmental numbers. Their bird observations are kept hidden so the organizers can evaluate your predictions. Your model produces a score for each of the ten target species at each location: 159,150 predictions in total.

Save the supplied identifiers and your scores in a CSV, a plain-text table with two columns: row_id,score. Include exactly the IDs from the decrypted sample submission, in any order. Scores must be finite numbers between 0 and 1, and the CSV must be at most 24 MB. Higher scores rank locations as more suitable relative to other locations; a score of 0.73 does not mean a 73% chance that the bird is present. German spatial patches: keep the landscape's layout

A patch is a small grid of environmental data cut from a larger map. The modeling tables summarize an area; patches keep the position of each cell. They let a model use the arrangement of the surrounding landscape as well as its overall characteristics.

Each location has two views: a detailed view covering 1.28 × 1.28 km, and a wider view covering 2.56 × 2.56 km. Choose these optional files if your approach needs that spatial arrangement. You can complete the challenge using the prepared tables alone.

Feature counts, labels and validation

Environmental inputs

The compact input has 289 predictors: AlphaEarth contributes 195, WorldCover 39, terrain 19 and historical OpenStreetMap 36. The starter joins the companion tables. AlphaEarth's 195 values comprise three 64-number vectors (the local 10 m cell and circular summaries within 100 m and 1,000 m), plus three coverage fractions describing available data. Circular summaries combine an area; they are not single pixels at coarser resolutions.

AlphaEarth uses the 2024 representation, WorldCover is from 2021, and OpenStreetMap is from January 2024. Individual embedding coordinates do not have standalone ecological meanings. Keep the separate source notices and follow the packaged schema for exact field and coverage definitions.

Bird labels and evaluation splits

The target tables contain 83,172 training labels and 19,770 German development labels. Another 74,142 training labels cover eleven optional helper species. Five source/block/season-matched recording-opportunity backgrounds accompany each selected positive. Missing labels remain unknown, not negative labels. The exploration archive covers 492 species identifiers and 15 datasets.

Report the shared German development comparison. Five spatial folds are optional checks of how results vary across areas. Respect the supplied train, validation and purged roles across target and helper models and any preprocessing learned from data.

Spatial files, decoding and storage

German patches cover 38,374 locations in 210 compressed NPZ shards, with masks and an index. The local patch has 64 channels × 128 × 128 cells at 10 m; the wider patch has 64 channels × 64 × 64 cells at 40 m, using Google's normalized overview. Both scales are supplied for every final location with matching channels, masks and focal offsets.

Decode valid int8 values using sign(q) × (q / 127.5)²; −128 means NoData. Never average the encoded int8 values directly. Follow the packaged spatial schema for normalization, masks, axes, focal cells and offsets. National GeoTIFFs retain their source coordinate reference system and south-up transforms; patches are north-up. The 87 German rasters use a Germany-plus-2 km buffer.

The German tables, patches and exploration mirror totals 34.30 GB. The full optional collection is about 196.90 GB. A table-only final model needs the 28.2 MB encrypted table archive; optional patches bring the core test component to 13.84 GB. Models trained from whole German rasters must compute final features from the supplied windows; continuous georeferenced test-region rasters are not supplied. Keep a tested table baseline as a fallback.

What does the prediction score measure?

Average precision (AP) measures how well your model ranks recorded bird observations above the supplied background examples. Higher is better. We calculate a score for each of the ten species, then average them equally. This average is called macro AP. Background examples are a comparison group, not proof that a bird was absent; unknown labels are excluded.

The public leaderboard and the final result

During the event, the leaderboard shows your chosen submission's AP on the public part of the test. This is provisional feedback. Final model points use the same chosen prediction on a geographically separate, hidden private part. Private scores and group details stay hidden until choices are locked, pending submissions are processed and organizers publish the results.

The AP leaderboard ranks prediction performance. The overall award adds the jury's points for insights, presentation and reproducibility. The jury does not select your best hidden result from earlier attempts.

How AP becomes model points

The supplied starter model is the fixed baseline for comparison. Matching its AP on the private test earns 15 out of 50 model points. Beating it by 0.15 AP earns all 50 points; smaller improvements earn proportionally fewer points. Scores below the baseline can earn 0–15 points. Another team's result does not change this scale.

Exact AP calculation, model-point formula and ties

Within each public or private partition, pool all scored countries for each species, calculate AP and average the ten species equally. The background ratio and eligibility rules are fixed. Unknown labels are excluded, not turned into negatives. Geographic identifiers and public/private membership are absent from test inputs. Submitted scores are ranking outputs, not calibrated probabilities that a bird occupies a location.

Let s be your chosen prediction's private AP, a = 1/6 the constant-score AP, and b the fixed baseline's AP on that same private split.

  • For s ≤ b: max(0, 15 × (s − a) / (b − a)) model points.
  • For s > b: min(50, 15 + 35 × (s − b) / 0.15) model points.

At or below constant-score AP, you earn zero model points. Points are capped at 50.

What you can conclude from the results

Bird records depend on where people look, what they detect and what they report. Coordinates can be less precise than the image pixels used by the model. A high prediction score does not prove that a bird occupies or breeds at a location.

The annual 2024 inputs support looking back at seasonal reported presence; they do not establish a forecast or a cause-and-effect relationship. AlphaEarth's pretraining already includes biodiversity supervision. Finding species in similar places alone does not prove that they interact. Explain these limitations when presenting your findings.

Technical notes, when you need them

Choosing tables, spatial patches or rasters

The prepared tables are a complete starting route. Try a regularized linear model or suitable tree model, and compare local versus wider environmental features. Make sure file joins are correct and preprocessing respects the training/development split.

Optional spatial patches preserve the landscape's arrangement. They have 64 environmental channels and validity masks, not RGB colors. Follow the documented decoding, normalization and focal offsets; ordinary color preprocessing and brightness augmentation do not automatically make sense. Measure memory use and final prediction time, and download the matching final patches if your model needs them. Keep a tested table model as a fallback.

The 100 m and 1 km features summarize circular neighborhoods; they are not single coarse pixels. Native vectors, context summaries, 10 m patches and 40 m patches describe different views of the environment. A park may be lost in a broad average, so compare views rather than assuming one resolution is best. Fine pixels do not remove uncertainty in bird coordinates.

The 148.57 GB German rasters are optional advanced inputs. Custom features must also be possible to produce from the anonymous final inputs. See the data page for sizes, formats, species and licenses.

Comparing models fairly and using other species

Keep a short experiment log: question, features, split, seed, runtime and result. Everyone reports the common German development score. Five-fold spatial cross-validation is optional; it is not required and earns no points just for being run. Fit learned preprocessing and helper stages inside the permitted training partitions and follow the same exclusions.

After model selection, you may refit on training plus development data. Declare it, retain the earlier held-out development score and keep the same final prediction interface.

Task 2 offers optional exploration of German records, such as coverage, sources or seasonality. Task 3 asks whether other species help prediction. For a cross-species claim, compare with and without helpers using identical target labels, backgrounds, splits and feature supports. Learn helpers within the permitted training partitions, using out-of-fold predictions or another construction that avoids leaking answers, and predict them from the supplied final inputs. Observed helper labels at final locations are not supplied. A well-controlled negative result can be useful.

Learning data covers Germany; final evaluation checks transfer to nearby Central Europe. Do not download additional bird observations, range maps or pretrained bird-distribution predictions. Additional environmental training data are allowed, but final predictions must be computable from the supplied anonymous inputs. Organizers will not prepare extra test features on request.
